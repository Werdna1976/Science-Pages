The Hang Time Lab

In Elastic Launched Glider you build a tiny balsa glider at home, test it and keep a flight log, then bring it to the tournament. There you get a 6-minute flight period in a gym. You shoot the glider straight up with a rubber band on a stick, it turns over at the top, and it circles slowly down. The judges time how long it stays in the air. This page explains the science of each part of a flight and has lab exhibits you can play with until it makes sense.

How the event works

Almost all the work happens before the tournament: building, test flying, trimming, and logging. On the day, you have a few minutes to show what your glider can do.

Before the tournament: build and test

Build up to two gliders from a kit, published plans, or your own design. Kits can't have joints already glued or surfaces already covered. Any material is allowed except boron fibers. Fly it at least 10 times and write each flight in your flight log. You also need to be able to answer questions about how you built it and how it works.

Check-in: the box and the scale

You hand over your glider in its measurement box, plus your launch handle and flight log. The box has no lid and its outside must fit in 32.0 × 24.0 × 47.0 cm (about 12.6 × 9.4 × 18.5 inches). The glider must sit completely inside it, ready to fly, without bending it to fit. The supervisor weighs it: more than 3.5 g and less than 10.0 g (a US nickel is 5 g). If their scale reads a little differently from yours, you can add a bit of clay on the spot.

The nose test

For safety the nose must be blunt. The supervisor pushes it into a lip balm cap (about 1.57 cm deep and 1.37 cm wide inside). It must not touch the bottom of the cap. A pointy nose fails. A rounded nose block or a blob of clay wider than the cap's opening passes.

The flight period

The clock starts when the supervisor hands your launch handle back. You get 6 minutes. Before each launch, say whether it's an official flight or a trim flight (a practice one). If you don't say, it counts as official. You get up to 5 official flights with one or both gliders. You can fix and adjust during the period, but the clock doesn't stop for repairs. A flight that starts before time runs out gets to finish.

How a flight is timed

The clock runs from the moment the glider leaves your hand until any part of it touches the floor, or it gets stuck somewhere it isn't flying (a girder, a basketball hoop), or the supervisor calls it. Usually three timers each time it and the middle time (the median) counts. If your glider collides with another team's, you can ask for a re-flight, and that one doesn't use up your 6 minutes.

Bring and wear

Up to 2 gliders, your launch handle(s), at least one measurement box, your flight log, and tools for repairs. The supervisor supplies the scale and the timers. Category B eye protection: safety glasses with side shields marked "Z87+" (goggles also work). Once you're in the flying area, only you and your partner can touch the gliders, and nobody can hand you anything or coach you. Full list in the checklist.

The launch handle

A stick with a rubber loop on the end, like a slingshot with one arm. Without the rubber it must be less than 1 m long (39 inches). The rubber can't be metal, has to stay touching the glider during the launch, and has to stay on the handle. If the rubber flies off with the glider, that flight's timing ends. You must launch standing on the floor: no ladders, no chairs.

Division B vs. C

This page is for Division B. You'll find Division C glider guides online from 2013 and 2014, and Division B ones from 2015, 2016, and 2019 to 2021. The physics is the same, but the size, mass, and scoring rules have changed, so always check numbers against the 2027 manual.

The launch: rubber to height Climb

Every flight starts with energy you put into the rubber. The higher the glider goes, the more time it has to come down. But the ceiling is a hard limit.

The science: energy changes form

When you stretch the rubber, you store elastic potential energy in it. Let go and it turns into kinetic energy (energy of motion): the glider leaves at 15 to 25 meters per second, about 35 to 55 mph. As it climbs, gravity slows it down and the motion turns into gravitational potential energy (energy of height). At the top it's almost stopped, and it has to turn over into a glide. That turn is called the transition.

🤚 Stretch→Elastic energy→Speed→Height→Glide time

Not all the energy makes it. The rubber wastes some as heat and spends some flinging itself forward. Drag (air pushing back) steals more on the way up, and it steals more from fast, light gliders.

The formulas, term by term

Stored energy ≈ ½ × k × x²

k is how stiff the rubber is (newtons per meter), and x is how far you stretch it (meters). Twice the stretch gives four times the energy.

Height (no air) = energy ÷ (m × g)

m is the glider's mass in kilograms (6 g = 0.006 kg), and g is 9.8 m/s², the pull of gravity.

Medium rubber with k = 15 N/m, stretched 0.6 m: ½ × 15 × 0.6 × 0.6 = 2.7 joules stored. If about a third reaches the glider (0.95 J), a 6 g glider could climb 0.95 ÷ (0.006 × 9.8) = 16 m with no air. Drag cuts that to about 7 m above your hand, so the peak is near 9 m (29 feet) off the floor. That's about right for a strong gym launch.

A lighter glider leaves faster, but fast things get hit harder by the air. A heavier glider leaves slower but plows through. So the lightest glider doesn't always climb highest. Where it really pays to be light is the glide, coming up next.

In a low gym, you'll often have more launch power than you need. Then the game is control: pull the same distance every time, and pick a stretch that gets you about 1 m below the ceiling. Hitting the ceiling costs time and can break the glider. Your flight log records peak height and rubber length so you can do this on purpose.

The glide: coming down slowly Descent

Most of your time aloft is the glide. The slower your glider sinks, the more seconds you get from every meter of height.

The science: lift, drag, and sink rate

A glider has no motor. It's always sliding downhill through the air, trading height for speed. Its wing pushes air down, and the air pushes the wing up: that's lift. Lift holds up the glider's weight, and drag tries to slow it.

The number that matters is the sink rate: how many meters it drops each second. Two things set it:

  • Wing loading: mass per wing area. A big wing on a light glider can fly slowly, and a slow glider sinks slowly.
  • Glide ratio (lift-to-drag, or L/D): how far forward it goes for each meter down. A smooth, thin, slightly curved wing beats a flat, rough one.

The formulas, term by term

Speed = √( 2 × W ÷ (ρ × S × CL) )

W is weight in newtons (mass in kg × 9.8). ρ ("rho") is air density, 1.2 kg/m³. S is wing area in m². CL is the lift coefficient, about 0.6 for a well-trimmed glider.

Sink rate = speed ÷ glide ratio

Time aloft ≈ climb time + height ÷ sink rate

A 6 g glider (W = 0.059 N) with 180 cm² of wing (0.018 m²): speed = √(2 × 0.059 ÷ (1.2 × 0.018 × 0.6)) = 3.0 m/s, about 7 mph. With a glide ratio of 8, it sinks 3.0 ÷ 8 = 0.38 m/s. From 7 m that's about 18 seconds of glide plus 1 to 2 seconds of climb.

Balance: where the clay goes Trim

A glider that's built perfectly can still stall, dive, or flip if it's balanced wrong. Balance and the tail angle work as a pair.

The science: two balance points

The center of gravity (CG) is the balance point: put a finger there and the glider sits level. You move it forward by adding clay to the nose.

The neutral point is where the lift from the wing and the tail adds up. Think of it as the point the air "holds" the glider. For a stable glider, the CG has to be in front of the neutral point. The gap between them is the static margin.

The tail (the stabilizer, or stab) is set at a slightly smaller angle than the wing, with its back edge tilted up a little. That difference is the decalage. A nose-heavy glider needs more of it to hold the nose up; a tail-heavy one needs less.

How it works in flight

  • Just right: the glider settles into one smooth speed and holds it.
  • Stall (too little nose weight or too much up tail): the nose rises, it slows, it drops, it speeds up, and it rises again. That bobbing is called porpoising, and each bob wastes height.
  • Dive (too much nose weight or not enough up tail): it flies fast and steep and hits the floor early.
  • Tumble (CG behind the neutral point): nothing holds the nose steady, so it flips or flutters down.

It's like a dart. The heavy point goes in front and the feathers in back. Put the weight in the back and the dart flips around. A glider is a dart that's trying to fly slowly.

Turning: stay in the gym Circle

A glider that flies straight hits a wall in a few seconds. You want a wide, gentle circle that fits the room.

The science: rudder and dihedral

The rudder (the back part of the tall fin) is set a few degrees to one side. That swings the tail and points the nose into a turn. As it turns, the glider banks (tips its wings), like a bike leaning into a corner.

Dihedral is the V shape of the wings when you look from the front. When the glider tips, the lower wing gets more lift and pushes it back level. Dihedral fights the bank, the rudder makes it.

If the rudder wins by too much, the bank keeps growing, the circle gets smaller and steeper, and the glider corkscrews into the floor. That's a spiral dive.

How to read the room

  • Circle about two thirds of the room's narrow width. It drifts a bit, so leave space.
  • Most catapult gliders climb turning one way and glide turning the other way. Righties usually launch with the glider banked to the right and glide in a left circle (or the opposite for lefties).
  • Tighter circles mean more bank, and more bank means faster sinking.
  • Don't add weight to one wing to make it turn. Use the rudder.

Think of a bike on a curvy path. Steering (rudder) makes you lean. Your balance (dihedral) keeps you from tipping over. Turn the handlebars too hard and you fall in.

Build, trim, and launch Device

How to make a glider that's legal, light, and tough enough to survive a season of launches.

🪵 Materials

  • Balsa is the usual wood: light, easy to cut, and strong for its weight. Weigh sheets at the hobby store and pick the lightest ones. Light wing balsa is about 5 to 7 lb per cubic foot (80 to 110 kg/m³). The NFFS balsa density calculator on the event page tells you from a sheet's size and mass.
  • Grain: A-grain bends easily (good for curved parts), C-grain is stiff and doesn't twist (good for flat wings and the stab). B-grain is in between.
  • Wing and tail: 1/16" (1.6 mm) or 3/32" (2.4 mm) sheet. Fuselage: a hard balsa or basswood stick, or a thin carbon rod.
  • Glue: thin CA ("super glue") sets fast, and acetone dissolves it if you need to re-set a part. Hobby cement (like Duco) is slower but more forgiving.
  • Anything goes except boron fibers. A kit is fine as long as nothing comes pre-glued or pre-covered.

🛠 Build steps

  1. Start from the NFFS "basic non-flapper glider" plans and tips (linked below), or a kit. Save fancy designs for glider number two.
  2. Cut the wing. Sand it into an airfoil: rounded front edge, thin tapered back edge, and the thickest point about a quarter of the way back.
  3. Crack the wing at the middle (or at the tips) and glue it with dihedral, propping the tips on a block.
  4. Sand the stab and fin thin. Light tails matter: every gram at the back needs grams of clay at the front.
  5. Glue the wing and stab to the fuselage, checking from the front and above that everything is square. Use a reinforcing patch where the wing sits.
  6. Add a launch peg or notch near the back, behind the wing, for the rubber.
  7. Shape a blunt nose block. Test it in a lip balm cap: it must not touch the bottom.
  8. Add clay until it balances near the middle of the wing. Weigh it: more than 3.5 g and less than 10.0 g, with a few tenths of a gram of margin.
  9. Check it fits fully in your measurement box. Write your names on it.

🎯 Launching like a pro

  • Hold the glider from above, just behind the wing, so your fingers don't touch the tail.
  • Hook the rubber on the launch peg. Hold the handle straight up and pull the glider straight down below it.
  • Tilt the whole setup about 70 to 80° from the floor (nearly straight up) and roll the glider about 45° onto its side. That bank is what makes it turn over at the top instead of looping backward.
  • Pull to a marked spot every time (a tape mark on your arm or a knot on a string) so launches are repeatable.
  • Control height by shortening the rubber: start loose, test launch in the gym, and wrap the loop around the handle until the peak sits about 1 m below the ceiling. Don't guess a new pull distance on the day.
  • Launch away from walls, lights, and basketball hoops, and wait for other gliders to clear.

🧰 Repair kit

  • Thin CA glue and a fine applicator tip
  • Clear tape and a few cm of light balsa scraps
  • Extra clay in a small container, and a toothpick to place it
  • Spare rubber loops, already cut and tied to your tested length
  • Small sandpaper, a hobby knife (adults only, or with permission), tweezers
  • A small ruler and your own digital scale (0.01 g if possible)

The clock keeps running during repairs, so practice quick fixes at home: a cracked wing taped in under a minute.

🦋 Flapper gliders (advanced)

A flapper glider has a wing or stab that's held flat by the rubber's pull during launch, then pops to its gliding angle at the top. It climbs straight and fast with no lift fighting it, then glides with full lift. They can fly much longer, but they're harder to build and trim. NFFS has separate build and trim guides for flappers, linked below. Learn on a basic glider first.

The flight log ×1.2

The bonus that comes from paperwork. Here's what the rules require, and a practice tool to spot patterns in your flights.

What a complete log needs

  • Data from 10 or more test flights made before the competition.
  • Three required items for each flight: (1) estimated or recorded peak height after launch, (2) approximate relaxed length of the rubber, and (3) flight time.
  • At least one more item of your choice. Good ones: clay added, stab tab setting, rudder setting, which glider, room, or what changed since the last flight.
  • Units on every number (s, m, cm, g).

The supervisor looks it over at check-in and gives it back. Bring it in a folder so it stays readable.

Estimating peak height

You can't hold a tape measure at the top of a launch. Use the room instead:

  • Count rows of concrete blocks: one row is about 20 cm (8 inches). Basketball rims are 3.05 m (10 ft).
  • Have your partner stand back and compare the peak with things whose height you know, like the hoop, a banner, or the bottom of the lights.
  • Phone video against a wall with height marks works well at home (phones aren't allowed at the event, but they're great for practice).

"Estimated" is allowed. Being consistent matters more than being perfect.

Game plan

How to practice, how to split the work with your partner, and what goes in your flight log.

🗓 A practice schedule

8+ weeks out: Read this page. Order balsa or a kit. Build glider #1 quickly, just to learn. Start the flight log with your very first flight.
6–8 weeks out: Trim #1 until it glides smoothly in a circle. Build glider #2 lighter and neater, using what you learned. Find a gym you can practice in.
4–6 weeks out: Tune the launch: mark your pull, set rubber length for your practice ceiling. Practice timing with three stopwatches and taking the middle time.
2–4 weeks out: Run full 6-minute flight periods with a timer: declare each flight, make 5 official flights. Practice a quick repair. Finish 10+ logged flights.
Last week: Check mass on your scale with a margin above 3.5 g. Test the nose in a lip balm cap. Check the box fits. Pack spares. Don't make big changes now.

👯 Splitting the work between partners

  • Partner A: pilot. Launches every flight the same way and makes trim changes.
  • Partner B: crew chief. Keeps the clock and the flight log, watches the flight from the side to estimate peak height, and calls "official" or "trim" before each launch.
  • At the tournament, B watches the 6-minute clock. Plan it: maybe 1 trim flight to check the room, then official flights about once a minute.
  • Both of you should be able to explain how the glider was built and trimmed.
  • Swap jobs at some practices, in case one of you is out sick.

📄 What to put in your flight log

  • A table: date, glider, flight time (s), peak height (m), relaxed rubber length (cm), plus your extra item (clay g, stab setting, room).
  • Ten or more rows, all before the tournament. Twenty is better.
  • A short note on what you changed and why ("added 0.05 g clay: it was stalling").
  • Glider specs: span, chord, wing area, mass, dihedral.
  • Your launch settings for different ceiling heights. That's your cheat sheet on the day.

There's no note sheet in this event. The flight log is your reference.

🦺 Practicing safely

  • Wear safety glasses every time you launch. Rubber can snap back and gliders come down fast.
  • Never launch toward a person, and shout "launching!" in a shared gym.
  • Hobby knives are sharp: cut away from your fingers on a cutting mat, with an adult nearby.
  • CA glue bonds skin in seconds. Work with ventilation and keep acetone nearby to unstick fingers.
  • Retrieving a glider stuck up high is a job for an adult with proper equipment, not a climb.
