How the event works

There's nothing to build at home. Everything happens at the tournament: a written test plus one or more short bench tasks. The supervisor brings all the parts and meters.

Part I: Written test (50–75%)

Questions on charges and static electricity, DC and AC electricity, current, voltage, resistance, power and energy, series and parallel circuits, open and short circuits, reading circuit diagrams, diodes and LEDs, digital logic (gates, truth tables, binary adding), control devices (switches, relays, fuses, breakers, GFCIs), and the scientists who figured it all out. At state and nationals there will be at least one Kirchhoff's law question.

Part II: Hands-on tasks (25–50%)

At least one task at a bench with a breadboard and a multimeter. Examples from the rules: find a mystery resistor using only voltage readings, work out the power in a circuit, make two LEDs glow equally bright, read a USB charger label and figure out charging time, build a logic circuit that matches a truth table, find the fault in a broken circuit, wire a 3-way light switch, or build a set resistance from given resistors. No soldering, no high voltage, no AC.

Bring

Notes of any size on paper, from any source: a binder, tabs, sheet protectors, even printed books. Two Class III calculators per team (see the manual's calculator definitions for what Class III allows). Pencils. Your own basic multimeter if the supervisor allows it, which is worth it because you'll know its buttons. No phones or smartwatches. Full list in the checklist.

Division B vs. C

This page is for Division B. B gets control devices (switches, relays, fuses, GFCIs, breakers), and its math stays simple: no trigonometry. Division C adds flip-flops, transistors, and op-amps. Neither division gets capacitors, inductors, magnets, Thevenin circuits, or oscilloscopes this year.

Ohm's law and power Written + bench

Four quantities run every circuit: voltage, current, resistance, and power. Learn what each one means and the two formulas that link them, and half the test opens up.

The big four

  • Voltage (V, in volts): the push. A 9 V battery pushes harder than a 1.5 V AA.
  • Current (I, in amperes or amps, A): how much charge flows past a point each second. 1 milliamp (mA) = 0.001 A.
  • Resistance (R, in ohms, Ω): how hard it is for current to get through. 1 kΩ = 1,000 Ω.
  • Power (P, in watts, W): how fast energy is used or turned into heat and light.

Think of a garden hose. Voltage is the water pressure, current is how many gallons per minute come out, and resistance is how much you pinch the hose. Pinch harder (more resistance) and less water flows, unless you turn up the pressure.

The two formulas

V = I × R

Ohm's law. V is volts, I is amps, R is ohms. Cover the one you want on the triangle in the exhibit: R = V ÷ I, I = V ÷ R.

P = I × V = I² × R = V² ÷ R

Power. All three versions give the same answer; pick the one that uses what you know.

A 1,500 W space heater on a 120 V outlet: I = P ÷ V = 1500 ÷ 120 = 12.5 A. Its resistance: R = V ÷ I = 120 ÷ 12.5 = 9.6 Ω.

Math you might see

  • Units: 1 kΩ = 1,000 Ω; 1 MΩ = 1,000,000 Ω; 1 mA = 0.001 A. Convert before you multiply. 9 V ÷ 2.2 kΩ = 9 ÷ 2200 = 0.0041 A = 4.1 mA.
  • Wire resistance: R = ρL ÷ A. ρ (rho) depends on the material, L is length, A is cross-section area. Longer wire = more resistance; thicker wire = less. Stretch a wire to twice its length and it also gets thinner, so its resistance goes up 4 times.
  • Efficiency = useful energy out ÷ energy in. A 1,500 W kettle at 90% heats water with 1,350 W. Heating 1 liter from 20 °C to 100 °C (68 °F to 212 °F) takes 1000 g × 4.18 J/g°C × 80 °C ≈ 334,000 J, so 334,000 ÷ 1,350 ≈ 248 s ≈ 4.1 minutes.
  • Charge: Q = I × t. 2 A for 10 s moves 20 coulombs.

DC and AC

DC (direct current) flows one way, like from a battery. AC (alternating current) flips direction back and forth, 60 times a second (60 Hz) in US wall outlets and 50 Hz in Europe.

The "120 V" on a US outlet is an RMS average. The voltage really swings up to a peak of about 120 × 1.414 ≈ 170 V and back down to −170 V.

Power companies send electricity at very high voltage because the power lost in the wires is I² × R. Raise the voltage 10 times and the current drops 10 times, so the loss drops 100 times.

AC circuit math isn't tested this year, but these facts about AC are.

Series and parallel circuits Written + bench

Every circuit is built from two patterns: parts in a line, or parts side by side. Learn how voltage and current behave in each and you can take apart any circuit on the test.

Series: one path

Parts in a single loop, one after another. The same current goes through every part. The battery's voltage gets shared between them, and the biggest resistor gets the biggest share.

R_total = R1 + R2 + R3

Break the loop anywhere (an open circuit) and everything stops, like old holiday lights.

Parallel: side by side

Each part gets its own path straight across the battery. Every branch gets the full voltage. The currents add up, and the smallest resistor takes the most current.

1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3

For just two: R_total = (R1 × R2) ÷ (R1 + R2). Two equal resistors in parallel give half of one. The total is always smaller than the smallest branch.

Kirchhoff's laws State & nationals

Voltage law (KVL): go all the way around any loop and the voltage rises and drops add up to zero. In a series loop, the drops across the parts add up to the battery voltage.

Current law (KCL): the current flowing into any junction equals the current flowing out. Charge doesn't pile up or vanish.

9 V battery, 100 Ω and 200 Ω in series. I = 9 ÷ 300 = 0.03 A = 30 mA. V1 = 0.03 × 100 = 3 V, V2 = 0.03 × 200 = 6 V. Check KVL: 3 + 6 = 9 ✓.

KCL: 50 mA flows into a junction that splits into two branches. One branch carries 20 mA, so the other must carry 50 − 20 = 30 mA.

Open and short circuits

  • Open: a gap in the path. No current flows in that path. In a series circuit, the whole battery voltage shows up across the gap. That's how you find a broken part with a voltmeter.
  • Short: a path with almost no resistance that skips part of the circuit. The skipped part gets zero volts. A short straight across a battery draws a huge current and gets hot. That's what fuses and breakers protect against.
  • Closed circuit: a complete loop, so current flows.

An open circuit is a drawbridge that's up: nobody gets across. A short circuit is a shortcut that's so easy everyone takes it and skips the town.

Voltage dividers

Two resistors in series split the voltage in proportion to their size:

V2 = V_battery × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)

9 V across 1 kΩ and 2 kΩ: the 2 kΩ gets 9 × 2 ÷ 3 = 6 V. This is the trick behind the "mystery resistor" bench task in the hands-on section.

Reading schematics

A schematic is a map of a circuit using symbols. Straight lines are wires (assume zero resistance). A zigzag is a resistor. A battery is a long line (+) and a short line (−). A dot where wires cross means they connect; no dot means they just pass over each other. Learn the symbols for switch, bulb, LED, diode, fuse, ground, voltmeter (V in a circle), and ammeter (A in a circle).

Diodes and LEDs New for 2027

A diode is a one-way valve for current. A light-emitting diode (LED) is a diode that glows. They don't follow Ohm's law, and that's exactly why they need a resistor.

How a diode works

A silicon diode is made of two kinds of silicon pressed together: P-type (with "holes" that act like positive charges) and N-type (with extra electrons). Where they meet is the PN junction.

  • Forward bias (+ to the P side, the anode): once the voltage passes about 0.7 V for silicon, current flows easily.
  • Reverse bias (the other way): almost no current flows, until a high enough voltage breaks it down.

The schematic symbol is an arrow pointing the way current flows, with a bar at the cathode end (−). On a real diode the cathode has a stripe. On an LED the longer leg is the anode (+) and the cathode side of the rim is flat.

Why an LED needs a resistor

Each LED color has a forward voltage: about 2 V for red, 2.1 V for yellow, and 3–3.3 V for blue, white, and bright green. Bluer light carries more energy per photon, so it needs a bigger push.

Below that voltage, almost nothing happens. A little above it, the current shoots up. Hook an LED straight to a 9 V battery and it pulls far more than its ~20 mA limit and burns out. A resistor in series soaks up the extra voltage:

R = (V_supply − V_LED) ÷ I

Red LED on a 9 V battery, aiming for 20 mA: R = (9 − 2) ÷ 0.020 = 350 Ω. Use the next bigger standard size, 390 Ω, to stay safe: I = 7 ÷ 390 = 18 mA.

Bench task: make two LEDs equally bright

Brightness follows current, so give both LEDs the same current.

  • Easiest: put them in series with one resistor. Same path, same current. You need enough supply voltage for both: two red LEDs need about 4 V plus a little for the resistor.
  • In parallel: give each LED its own resistor. If two different LEDs share one resistor in parallel, the one with the lower forward voltage hogs nearly all the current.
  • Different colors at the same current can still look different to your eye. Match the current and say so in your answer.

Diode facts to know

  • Silicon diode drop: about 0.7 V when conducting. Germanium: about 0.3 V.
  • A diode in a battery holder stops damage if someone puts the batteries in backwards.
  • LEDs are far more efficient than old bulbs: a 9 W LED gives about the light of a 60 W bulb, which wasted most of its energy as heat.
  • Most LEDs survive only about 5 V backwards before breaking down.

Digital logic New for 2027

Computers are built from tiny switches that only know two states: on (1) or off (0). Logic gates combine those 1s and 0s to make decisions. Division B covers gates, truth tables, Boolean expressions, De Morgan's laws, binary numbers, multiplexers, and adders.

In Boolean notation · means AND, + means OR, a prime (A′) or a bar over a letter means NOT, and ⊕ means XOR.

Truth tables and Boolean expressions

A truth table lists every possible combination of inputs and the output for each. Two inputs make 2 × 2 = 4 rows; three inputs make 2³ = 8 rows.

A Boolean expression writes the same thing as algebra. Q = A·B + C′ means "Q is on when A and B are both on, or when C is off."

Q = A·B + C′ with A = 1, B = 0, C = 0: A·B = 1·0 = 0. C′ = NOT 0 = 1. Q = 0 + 1 = 1.

De Morgan's laws and universal gates

(A·B)′ = A′ + B′    (A+B)′ = A′·B′

"Break the bar, change the sign." NOT of an AND is the same as OR-ing the NOTs. So a NAND gate is the same as an OR gate with both inputs flipped.

NAND and NOR are universal: you can build every other gate from just NANDs (or just NORs). A NAND with both inputs tied together is a NOT. That's why chip makers love them.

Multiplexers

A multiplexer (MUX) is a data selector: it has several data inputs and a select input that picks which one passes through to the output, like a TV remote picking a channel.

2-to-1 MUX: Q = S′·A + S·B

When S = 0 the output copies A; when S = 1 it copies B. A 4-to-1 MUX needs 2 select lines (2² = 4 choices).

Binary numbers

Binary uses only 0 and 1. Each place is worth twice the one to its right: 8, 4, 2, 1.

1011₂ = 8 + 0 + 2 + 1 = 11. To convert 13 to binary: 13 = 8 + 4 + 1 → 1101.

Adding: 0+0 = 0, 0+1 = 1, 1+1 = 10 (write 0, carry 1), 1+1+1 = 11 (write 1, carry 1). Division B uses whole numbers only, with no negatives.

Each column is one full adder: Sum = A ⊕ B ⊕ Carry-in, and it carries out when at least two of its three inputs are 1.

Charges and static electricity Written

Before currents, there are charges. Like charges push apart, opposite charges pull together, and the force gets weaker fast as they move apart.

Charge basics

  • Charge is measured in coulombs (C). One electron carries −1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, so 1 C is about 6.2 billion billion electrons.
  • Objects get charged by gaining or losing electrons. Protons stay put in the nucleus.
  • Charge is conserved: rub a balloon on your hair, and the electrons the balloon gains are exactly the ones your hair loses.
  • Conductors (metals, salt water) let charge move easily. Insulators (rubber, plastic, glass, dry air) don't.

Three ways to charge something

  • Friction: rubbing moves electrons from one material to the other. The triboelectric series ranks which materials grab electrons.
  • Conduction: touch a charged object to a neutral one and some charge flows over. Both end up with the same sign.
  • Induction: bring a charged object near (not touching) a conductor. Its charges shift. Ground the far side, remove the ground, then move away, and the conductor keeps the opposite charge.

An electroscope (two thin leaves of foil in a jar) shows charge: the leaves spread apart because they get the same sign and repel.

Uses k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² for point charges in air. 1 newton ≈ 0.225 pounds of force.

Charges of +4 µC and −3 µC, 30 cm apart. Convert first: q₁ = 4 × 10⁻⁶ C, q₂ = 3 × 10⁻⁶ C, r = 0.30 m.

F = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (4 × 10⁻⁶) × (3 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ 0.30² = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 1.2 × 10⁻¹¹ ÷ 0.09 ≈ 1.2 N, a pull (opposite signs). Move them to 60 cm (twice as far) and the force drops to a quarter: 0.3 N.

The r² is the same "inverse square" pattern as gravity and light from a lamp. Twice as far = ¼ the force. Three times as far = 1/9. Half as far = 4 times the force.

Hands-on bench skills 25–50%

The bench tasks use a breadboard, resistors, LEDs, batteries, and a multimeter. These exhibits drill the skills that come up again and again.

How a breadboard is wired inside

  • The middle area has short rows of 5 holes (a–e on one side, f–j on the other). The 5 holes in a row are connected by a metal clip underneath.
  • The center gap separates the two halves, so a chip can straddle it without shorting its pins together.
  • The long power rails along the edges (red + and blue −) run the length of the board. On some long boards the rails are split in the middle; check with your meter.
  • Two legs of the same part in the same row = that part is shorted out. It's the most common breadboard mistake.

Multimeter rules of thumb

  • Voltage: probes across the part (in parallel), circuit on.
  • Current: break the circuit and put the meter in the path (in series), and move the red lead to the "A" or "mA" jack.
  • Resistance: power off, and ideally take the part out of the circuit.
  • Black lead in COM. Start on a high range and work down if your meter isn't auto-ranging.
  • "OL" means over limit: the value is bigger than the range, or the path is open.

The mystery resistor task: a 9 V battery, a known R1 = 1,000 Ω, and an unknown R2 in series. You're allowed only voltage readings.

You read V1 = 3.10 V across R1 and V2 = 5.90 V across R2. In series the current is the same in both, so I = V1 ÷ R1 = 3.10 ÷ 1000 = 0.00310 A. Then R2 = V2 ÷ I = 5.90 ÷ 0.00310 ≈ 1,900 Ω. Shortcut: R2 = R1 × V2 ÷ V1. Check: 3.10 + 5.90 = 9.00 V ✓.

Bench task: build a resistance from parts

Given three 100 Ω resistors, you can make:

  • 300 Ω: all three in series.
  • 33.3 Ω: all three in parallel (100 ÷ 3).
  • 150 Ω: one in series with two in parallel (100 + 50).
  • 66.7 Ω: two in series (200) in parallel with one (100): 200 × 100 ÷ 300.

Measure the real resistors first: a "100 Ω" 5% resistor can be anywhere from 95 to 105 Ω.

Bench task: read a USB charger label

A label says Output: 5V ⎓ 2A. (⎓ is the symbol for DC.) Power = 5 × 2 = 10 W.

A phone battery says 3.7 V, 3000 mAh. Energy = 3.7 V × 3.0 Ah = 11.1 Wh.

Charging time ≈ 11.1 Wh ÷ 10 W ≈ 1.1 hours if nothing were wasted. With about 80% efficiency: 11.1 ÷ (10 × 0.8) ≈ 1.4 hours. Using watt-hours avoids a trap: the charger runs at 5 V but the battery at 3.7 V, so you can't just divide mAh by mA.

Bench task: find the fault

  1. Check the battery voltage first. A dead battery is the most common "fault."
  2. Measure the voltage across each part with the power on.
  3. In a series circuit, a part with the full battery voltage across it is open (broken).
  4. A part with 0 V across it while others work is shorted (or it's a wire).
  5. On a breadboard, check for parts in the wrong row or a missing jumper.

Try both faults in the circuit board exhibit and watch R2's voltage.

Measuring like a pro

  • Write every reading with its unit, and the range the meter was on.
  • Record what the meter shows, digit for digit. Don't round until the final answer.
  • Check your work with Kirchhoff: do the voltages add up to the battery?
  • A negative voltage reading just means the probes are swapped.
  • Batteries sag under load. Measure the battery while the circuit is connected.
  • Pinching both resistor leads with your fingers adds your body's resistance in parallel. Hold just one side.

Switches and relays

  • Pole = how many separate circuits a switch controls. Throw = how many positions each pole can connect to.
  • SPST (single pole, single throw): plain on/off, like a light switch.
  • SPDT: one input, two outputs. Used in 3-way switches.
  • DPDT: two SPDTs flipped together. Can reverse a motor.
  • Momentary push buttons: normally open (NO) or normally closed (NC).
  • Relay: a switch flipped by electricity instead of a finger. A small current in a coil pulls a contact closed, so a tiny circuit can switch a big one.

Protection devices

  • Fuse: a thin wire that melts if too much current flows, opening the circuit. Replace it after it blows. Your multimeter has one in its amps jack.
  • Circuit breaker: does the same job but trips a switch, so you can reset it. Most home circuits are 15 or 20 A.
  • GFCI (ground fault circuit interrupter): compares the current going out on the hot wire with the current coming back on neutral. If about 5 mA is missing (maybe going through a person to ground), it cuts power in a fraction of a second. Required near water: kitchens, bathrooms, outdoors.
  • Ground wire: the third prong. Gives stray current a safe path so a metal case can't become live.

Electrical safety

It's the current through your body that hurts, and voltage is what pushes it through your skin's resistance. Wet skin has much lower resistance, which is why water and electricity are dangerous together.

Current through bodyRough effect
About 1 mAA tingle you can feel
About 10–20 mAMuscles clamp; you may not be able to let go
About 100 mA and upCan stop the heart's rhythm

Numbers vary by person and path. That's why GFCIs trip at around 5 mA.

2–4 weeks out: Timed practice tests (skip 2026 questions on magnets and capacitors). Add every missed idea to your binder.
Last week: Fresh battery in your meter. Tab your binder. Flashcards and one full timed run with your partner.

👯 Splitting 50 minutes between two partners

  • Decide roles ahead of time. One common split: Partner A starts the bench task while Partner B starts the written test, then they swap or meet in the middle.
  • The bench partner should be the one who's practiced the meter most. The written partner owns logic and history.
  • Pull the test apart if the supervisor allows it.
  • Save the last 5 minutes to check each other's math and units. A blank answer is always wrong; a guess might be right.

📒 What to put in your binder

  • Page 1: every formula with units (Ohm's law, power, energy, series and parallel, divider, Coulomb, R = ρL/A).
  • The resistor color code with a worked example.
  • LED forward voltages by color and the resistor formula.
  • All seven gate symbols with truth tables; De Morgan's laws; a 2-to-1 MUX; half and full adder tables.
  • Schematic symbols; switch types (SPST, SPDT, DPDT); fuse, breaker, GFCI.
  • The scientists table and key dates.
  • Your own multimeter's dial: which jack, which setting for each job.

Any amount of paper is allowed, but you only have 50 minutes. Tabs and a one-page formula sheet up front beat a thick, messy binder.

🦺 Practicing safely at home

  • Stick to batteries and USB power. Never experiment with wall outlets.
  • Never short a battery's terminals with a wire. Lithium and 9 V batteries can get hot enough to burn.
  • Resistors can get hot. Check the power: a common resistor is rated ¼ W.
  • Don't look straight into a bright LED up close.
  • Store loose batteries so their ends can't touch keys or coins.
