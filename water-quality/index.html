There's no lab experiment to cook up this year. It's mostly a written test, plus one hands-on part with a gadget you make at home. That gadget is the only part you can finish before you walk in the door.

Part I: Estuaries and oceans 30%

Where rivers meet the sea, how the ocean is layered, and how energy and pollution move through food webs.

What's an estuary?

An estuary is a partly enclosed place where a river's fresh water mixes with salty ocean water: bays, lagoons, sounds, and river mouths. The water is brackish (somewhere between fresh and salty), and its saltiness changes with the tides every day.

Estuaries are nurseries. Most of the fish and shellfish people catch in the US (salmon, herring, oysters, crabs) spend part of their lives in one. Marsh grasses, mangroves, and oyster reefs filter out mud and pollution before it reaches the ocean, and they soften storm waves. And 22 of the world's 32 biggest cities sit on estuaries, which is why they're under so much pressure.

Picture a river running into the ocean. Fresh water is lighter than salt water, so it tends to float on top. How much the two mix depends on a tug-of-war between the river pushing out and the tides sloshing in and out. Try it in the exhibit below.

Two ways to sort estuaries

By how they formed (geology):

  • Coastal plain (drowned river valley): sea level rose and flooded a river valley. Chesapeake Bay.
  • Bar-built: sandbars or barrier islands wall off a lagoon. North Carolina's Outer Banks.
  • Fjord: a deep U-shaped valley carved by a glacier, flooded by the sea. Alaska, Norway.
  • Tectonic: the ground sank or cracked along a fault. San Francisco Bay.
  • Delta: a river drops so much sediment it builds new land in fingers. Mississippi.

By how the water mixes:

  • Salt wedge: strong river, weak tides. Fresh water on top, a wedge of salt water along the bottom. Mississippi, Columbia.
  • Partially mixed: medium of both. Saltier at the bottom, but blended. Puget Sound, San Francisco Bay.
  • Well mixed: strong tides, weak river. Same saltiness top to bottom, getting saltier toward the sea. Delaware Bay.
  • Fjord type: deep basin with a shallow ridge (sill) at the mouth, so deep water barely gets swapped out.

Salinity is measured in ppt (parts per thousand, grams of salt in a kilogram of water). River water is near 0, the open ocean is about 35. Turn the river up and the tides down and you get a sharp line between a fresh top and a salty bottom: a salt wedge. Turn the tides up and that line smears out until the whole water column is the same.

Why it matters for living things: a bottom-dwelling oyster in a salt wedge estuary may sit in salty water while fish swimming above it are in nearly fresh water. A sharp layer also blocks oxygen from mixing down, which is how dead zones form at the bottom.

Estuary habitats

  • Salt marshes: grassy flats flooded by tides (cordgrass, Spartina). Temperate coasts.
  • Mangrove forests: salt-tolerant trees on stilt roots. Tropical coasts. Fish nurseries and storm barriers.
  • Seagrass beds (submerged aquatic vegetation): underwater meadows. Food for manatees and sea turtles; need clear water.
  • Mudflats and sandflats: uncovered at low tide, full of worms and clams that shorebirds eat.
  • Oyster reefs: one adult oyster filters up to about 50 gallons of water a day.
  • Barrier beaches and islands: shield the estuary behind them from waves.
  • Kelp forests and rocky shores: at the salty, cold ocean end of some estuaries.
  • Tidal creeks: channels that carry water in and out of marshes twice a day.

Living things here are adapted to changing salt levels. Animals that handle a wide range of salinity are euryhaline (salmon, blue crabs); ones that need steady salinity are stenohaline (most corals).

The ocean, top to bottom

Zones to know

  • Intertidal (littoral): between high and low tide. Wet, then dry, twice a day.
  • Neritic: the shallow water over the continental shelf. Most of the ocean's life and fishing is here.
  • Oceanic: the open ocean past the shelf.
  • Pelagic = the open water itself. Benthic = the sea floor.
  • Depth layers: epipelagic (0–200 m, sunlit, the photic zone), mesopelagic (200–1,000 m, twilight), bathypelagic (1,000–4,000 m, dark), abyssopelagic (4,000–6,000 m), hadal (trenches).

Who lives where

  • Plankton: drifters that can't swim against currents. Phytoplankton (plant-like, make food from sunlight) and zooplankton (tiny animals). The most numerous living things in the sea and the base of the food web.
  • Nekton: strong swimmers: fish, squid, whales, turtles.
  • Benthos: bottom dwellers: crabs, clams, worms, sea stars, corals.

Almost all photosynthesis happens in the top 200 m, because that's as far as useful sunlight reaches. That's why the open ocean is a bit of a desert while shallow coasts are packed with life.

Moving water: tides, currents, upwelling

  • Tides come from the Moon's (and Sun's) pull. Most coasts get two highs and two lows a day.
  • Spring tides (biggest range) happen at full and new moon when Sun and Moon line up. Neap tides (smallest) happen at quarter moons.
  • Surface currents are pushed by wind and bent by the Earth's spin into big loops called gyres.
  • Thermohaline circulation ("the global conveyor belt") is driven by differences in temperature and salt: cold, salty water is dense and sinks near the poles.
  • Upwelling: wind pushes surface water away from a coast, and cold, nutrient-rich deep water rises to replace it. Upwelling zones (Peru, California) are some of the best fishing spots on Earth.
  • El Niño: the trade winds weaken, warm water sloshes east across the Pacific, and upwelling off South America shuts down. Fish catches crash, and reefs can bleach from the heat.
  • La Niña is the opposite: stronger winds, more upwelling, cooler eastern Pacific.

Food webs and the 10% rule

Each step up a food chain is a trophic level: producers (phytoplankton, seagrass) → primary consumers (zooplankton, grazers) → secondary consumers (small fish) → tertiary consumers (big fish) → top predators (sharks, dolphins, ospreys). Only about 10% of the energy at one level makes it to the next. The rest is used for living (moving, staying warm) or lost as heat and waste.

A sample test question: "If producers make 50,000 J of energy, how much reaches secondary consumers?"
Primary consumers get 10%: 50,000 × 0.1 = 5,000 J. Secondary consumers get 10% of that: 5,000 × 0.1 = 500 J. Count the steps carefully: producers are level 1, so secondary consumers are two jumps up.

Living together

  • Mutualism (both win): coral and zooxanthellae; cleaner shrimp and fish.
  • Commensalism (one wins, other doesn't care): barnacles riding on a whale.
  • Parasitism (one wins, one loses): sea lice on salmon.
  • Predation and competition (fighting over food or space).
  • A keystone species has a huge effect for its numbers. Sea otters eat urchins; without otters, urchins eat the kelp forests.

Population growth

G = r · N

G is how many individuals the population grows by per unit of time, r is the growth rate, and N is how many there are now.

A bay has 2,000 oysters and r = 0.15 per year. G = 0.15 × 2,000 = 300 new oysters this year. Populations can't grow forever: food, space, and predators set a ceiling called the carrying capacity.

Cycles

💧 Water cycle

Evaporation → condensation (clouds) → precipitation → runoff and infiltration (soaking into groundwater) → back to the sea. Plants add water vapor by transpiration. About 97% of Earth's water is in the oceans; most fresh water is frozen in ice caps and glaciers.

🌱 Nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon

  • Nitrogen: bacteria turn N₂ gas into ammonia (nitrogen fixation), then into nitrite and nitrate (nitrification), and back to N₂ gas (denitrification, needs low oxygen).
  • Phosphorus: no gas phase. It comes from rocks, soil, fertilizer, and sewage, and ends up in sediment.
  • Carbon: the ocean soaks up about a quarter of the CO₂ people put into the air. That's good for the climate but makes the ocean more acidic (see Part III).

Too many nutrients: eutrophication and dead zones

🚜 Fertilizer and sewage wash into the water→ 🟢 Algae bloom→ ☁️ Water gets cloudy, seagrass gets shaded out→ 💀 Algae die and sink→ 🦠 Bacteria decompose them and use up oxygen (BOD rises)→ 🐟 Oxygen drops below 2 mg/L: a dead zone

The Gulf of Mexico gets a dead zone every summer from fertilizer carried down the Mississippi. In salt water, nitrogen is usually the nutrient in short supply (so adding it causes blooms); in fresh water it's usually phosphorus.

Human impacts

  • Harmful algal blooms (HABs): some algae make toxins. "Red tide" (Karenia brevis in Florida) kills fish and makes people cough on the beach; toxins in shellfish can poison people.
  • Sediment from construction, farms, and dredging clouds water, smothers coral and oysters, and carries pollutants along.
  • Oil spills coat feathers and fur and poison eggs and larvae.
  • Heavy metals (mercury, lead, cadmium) and PCBs build up in food webs: bioaccumulation (in one animal over its life) and biomagnification (higher at each trophic level).
  • TBT (tributyltin), an old boat-hull paint, made female snails grow male parts (imposex). It's now banned on most ships.
  • Emerging contaminants: medicines, microplastics, and PFAS ("forever chemicals" from nonstick pans and firefighting foam) that water treatment doesn't fully remove.
  • Invasive species: lionfish in the Caribbean eat reef fish; green crabs on both US coasts; zebra and quagga mussels in the Great Lakes. Often carried in ships' ballast water. Invasives are the second-biggest threat to biodiversity after habitat loss.
  • The five ocean garbage patches sit in the five big gyres: North Pacific (the "Great Pacific Garbage Patch," between Hawaii and California), South Pacific, North Atlantic, South Atlantic, and Indian Ocean. They're mostly tiny bits of plastic, not a floating island.
  • Overfishing and bycatch (animals caught by accident, like turtles in shrimp nets).

Cleaning water

🚰 Drinking water treatment

  1. Coagulation and flocculation: add alum so tiny particles clump into "floc."
  2. Sedimentation: the floc settles to the bottom.
  3. Filtration: through sand, gravel, and charcoal.
  4. Disinfection: chlorine, chloramine, UV light, or ozone kills germs.

Ordinary filters don't remove dissolved salt. Turning seawater into drinking water (desalination) needs reverse osmosis or distillation.

🚽 Wastewater (sewage) treatment

  1. Preliminary: screens catch rags and sticks; grit settles out.
  2. Primary: sewage sits in tanks; solids sink as sludge, grease floats off.
  3. Secondary: bacteria eat the dissolved organic matter (activated sludge). Must remove 85% of the BOD.
  4. Tertiary: extra cleanup to strip out nitrogen and phosphorus, then disinfection before release.

A watershed is all the land that drains into one body of water. What happens on land anywhere in it ends up in the estuary.

Part II: Coral reefs 30%

A coral is an animal with tiny algae living inside it, building itself a house of rock. Learn how that partnership works, what breaks it, and the 23 reef organisms on the list.

What is a coral?

A coral is a colony of tiny animals called polyps, relatives of jellyfish and sea anemones. Each polyp is a little tube with a ring of stinging tentacles. Reef-building (hermatypic) corals pull calcium and carbonate out of seawater to build a skeleton of calcium carbonate (aragonite) under themselves. Over thousands of years, those skeletons pile up into a reef.

Inside each polyp live zooxanthellae, single-celled algae (dinoflagellates). It's mutualism: the algae photosynthesize and give the coral up to 90% of its food plus oxygen, and they give coral most of its color. The coral gives the algae shelter, CO₂, and nitrogen and phosphorus from its waste.

Corals reproduce by budding and breaking apart (asexual), and many species also spawn all at once on a few nights a year, releasing clouds of eggs and sperm. The baby corals (planulae) drift, then settle on hard bottom and start a new colony.

What reefs need

  • Warm, steady water: about 23–29 °C (73–84 °F).
  • Lots of light, so shallow (most reefs are less than 50 m deep).
  • Clear water: low turbidity and little sediment.
  • Low nutrients: extra nutrients feed seaweed that crowds coral out.
  • Normal ocean salinity (about 32–40 ppt), no big floods of fresh water.
  • A hard surface to attach to, and some water movement.

Reef types: fringing reefs hug the shore; barrier reefs are separated from shore by a lagoon (the Great Barrier Reef); atolls are rings of reef around a lagoon where a volcanic island sank.

Reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor but are home to about a quarter of all ocean species. They protect coasts from waves, feed millions of people, and bring in tourism money.

Coral bleaching

When water stays too hot for too long, the zooxanthellae start making harmful chemicals, and the coral spits them out. Without its algae, the coral turns white (you see the skeleton through clear tissue) and starts starving. If the heat ends soon, the algae can move back in. If not, the coral dies and gets covered in seaweed. Heat is the main cause, but cold snaps, fresh water floods, pollution, and too much sunlight can also cause bleaching.

NOAA's Coral Reef Watch tracks heat stress in degree heating weeks (DHW): add up how many degrees above the normal summer maximum the water has been, for each week, over the last 12 weeks. Only weeks at least 1 °C too hot count.

Other threats

  • Ocean acidification: more CO₂ makes seawater less able to give corals the carbonate they need for skeletons (see aragonite in Part III).
  • Coral diseases: white band disease (wiped out much of the Caribbean's elkhorn and staghorn coral) and stony coral tissue loss disease (SCTLD, spreading through Florida and the Caribbean since 2014).
  • Crown-of-thorns starfish outbreaks: these starfish eat coral. Nutrient runoff helps their larvae survive, so outbreaks follow pollution.
  • Overfishing: take out the parrotfish and urchins that graze algae and seaweed takes over.
  • Sediment and nutrient runoff from land.
  • Physical damage: boat anchors, careless divers, collecting for souvenirs and aquariums, and destructive fishing with dynamite or cyanide.

How scientists check a reef's health

Reef Check trains volunteer divers to swim a straight line (a transect) and count things. Their checklist uses indicator organisms: species that are easy to spot and that reveal a problem when they're missing or too common. Fewer big groupers or parrotfish = overfishing. Lots of fleshy seaweed = too many nutrients or too few grazers. Fewer butterflyfish = less live coral.

They also measure coral cover (percent of the bottom that's live coral), recently killed coral, bleaching, trash, and fish size and biomass. Healthy reefs have plenty of apex predators (big groupers, sharks).

Protection: marine protected areas and no-take zones, fishing limits, mooring buoys instead of anchors, cleaner runoff, and growing coral in nurseries to replant.

Dissolved oxygen: why warm, salty water is a problem

Oxygen from the air dissolves into water, and algae add more by photosynthesis. But water can only hold so much. Cold water holds more oxygen than warm water, and fresh water holds more than salt water. That's why summer heat waves, especially in salty, still water, cause fish kills.

Saturation values use the standard oxygen solubility equation (APHA) at sea-level air pressure.

% saturation = measured DO ÷ the most the water can hold × 100.
Water at 25 °C and 35 ppt can hold about 6.8 mg/L. Your meter reads 5.5 mg/L. 5.5 ÷ 6.8 × 100 = 81% saturated. Fine for fish. The same 5.5 mg/L in 10 °C fresh water (which can hold 11.3) would be only 49%, which tells you something is using up the oxygen.

pH and ocean acidification

pH measures how acidic water is. Below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is basic. Each step is 10 times: pH 7 is 10× more acidic than pH 8, and 100× more than pH 9.

When CO₂ dissolves in seawater it makes carbonic acid:

CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

The extra H⁺ grabs carbonate ions (CO₃²⁻) that corals, clams, and some plankton need to build shells. Ocean pH has dropped from about 8.2 to 8.1 since the 1700s. That sounds tiny, but it's about 30% more acidic.

Aragonite saturation (Ω) tells you how easy it is to build shells. Ω above 1 means shells can form; Ω below 1 means they start to dissolve. Reefs grow best above 4.

Math you might see

  • pH and pOH: pH + pOH = 14. pH = −log[H⁺].
  • Salinity from chlorinity: S = 1.80655 × Cl (both in ppt). If Cl = 19.2 ppt, S ≈ 34.7 ppt.
  • BOD: DO on day 0 − DO after 5 days in the dark at 20 °C. 8.0 − 3.5 = 4.5 mg/L.
  • Secchi depth: lower the disk until it disappears, raise it until it reappears, and average the two depths. Light reaches about 2.7 × that depth. Smaller depth = murkier water.
  • Total solids = dissolved solids + suspended solids.
  • Percent and unit conversions: 35 ppt = 3.5% = 35 g/kg = 35,000 ppm.

Germs: fecal coliform and friends

Fecal coliform bacteria live in the guts of warm-blooded animals. They're mostly harmless themselves, but finding them means poop got in the water, and the germs that cause stomach bugs, hepatitis, and ear infections may be there too. Counts are reported per 100 mL. For ocean beaches, the EPA prefers testing for enterococci, which survive better in salt water.

Reading data tables and graphs

  • Look for things that move together: hot afternoon → low DO; heavy rain → low salinity, high turbidity and coliform.
  • DO often swings daily: high in the afternoon (algae photosynthesize), lowest just before dawn (everything respires all night).
  • Always read the units and axis labels first. mg/L vs % saturation and °C vs °F are easy traps.
  • Process skills count: independent vs dependent variable, controls, and whether the data supports a conclusion.

Part IV: Build a salinometer 10 points

The only points you can lock in before the tournament. Five for bringing a working one, five for getting the mystery sample right.

How it works

Salt water is denser (heavier for its size) than fresh water, so it pushes up harder on things floating in it. That's buoyancy. A floating object sinks until it pushes aside water weighing as much as itself. In denser, saltier water it doesn't have to sink as far, so it floats higher.

A float that measures density is called a hydrometer. Mark where the water line sits in known salt solutions and you've made a salinometer.

It's like floating in a swimming pool versus floating in the ocean. You bob higher in the ocean because salty water pushes up harder. Your straw does the same thing, and you measure how high it bobs.

The rules also allow electronic salinometers that measure how well the water conducts electricity, as long as you build it and it has nothing programmable and no Wi-Fi. A float is simpler and plenty accurate.

Build it

  1. You need: a clear plastic straw, modeling clay, a fine-tip permanent marker, a tall clear glass or graduated cylinder, salt, water (distilled is best), and a kitchen scale.
  2. Plug one end of the straw with clay, pressed in tight with no air bubbles and no leaks.
  3. Float it clay-end down in plain water. Add or pinch off clay until it floats straight up with most of the straw underwater.
  4. Mark the water line: that's 0%.
  5. Float it in a 10% standard and mark that line: 10%.
  6. Float it in more standards (2, 4, 6, 8%) and mark each one. Real marks beat guessed ones.
  7. Label your marks, write your names on it, and pack it in a tube so it can't get bent.

Upgrade: turn a plastic transfer pipette upside down, put sand in the bulb for weight, and seal it. The fat bulb with a thin stem spreads your marks much farther apart. Try both in the exhibit below.

The trick to a good salinometer is big gaps between marks. If 0% and 10% are only 9 mm apart, each 1% is less than a millimeter and you can't read it to 0.5%. Two things spread the marks out: having more of the float underwater (heavier, longer) and having a thin stem on top of a fat bottom (the pipette design). That's exactly how the glass hydrometers in science labs are shaped.

Use plain (non-iodized) salt; the supervisor will most likely use NaCl. A scale that reads to 0.1 g helps a lot.

Reading it like a pro

  • Make sure it floats straight up and isn't touching the side of the beaker.
  • Tap it gently to knock off air bubbles, which make it float too high.
  • Get your eye level with the water line. Read the bottom of the curve (the meniscus).
  • Rinse and dry it between samples so salt doesn't crust on and add weight.
  • Calibrate at room temperature. Warm water is a little less dense and reads low.
  • Re-check your 0% mark the morning of the tournament. Clay can shift or soak up water.
  • Estimate to the nearest tenth. If the line is a third of the way from 4 to 6, say 4.7.
  • Bring spare clay and your calibration numbers on your note sheet.

Game plan

How to practice, how to split up the test with your partner, and what to put on your note sheet.

🗓 A practice schedule

8+ weeks out: Read this page. Build a first salinometer this week, just to learn what goes wrong.
6–8 weeks out: Make proper standards and build your real salinometer. Test it on a sample a parent mixes without telling you the answer.
4–6 weeks out: One test part per week: estuaries and oceans, then reefs, then water tests. Learn the 23 organisms with quiz mode.
2–4 weeks out: Take practice tests (use 2027 marine and estuary ones, not 2026 freshwater ones). Make your note sheet from the questions you missed.
Last week: Re-check your salinometer marks. Pack it safely. Review flashcards.

👯 Splitting 50 minutes between two partners

  • Divide the test before the tournament. One common split: Partner A owns Part I and Part III; Partner B owns Part II (reefs and organism IDs) and the salinometer reading.
  • Pull the test apart if the supervisor allows it, so you both work at once.
  • Do the salinometer reading early when the station is free, so it's not rushed at the end.
  • Save the last 5 minutes to swap and check each other's answers. A blank answer is always wrong; a guess might be right.

📄 What to put on your note sheet

  • The parameter table (healthy values and units) from Part III.
  • Estuary types by geology and by mixing, with one example each.
  • Ocean zones with depths; plankton, nekton, benthos.
  • The 23 organisms with one identifying feature and what each one indicates.
  • NOAA bleaching alert levels; aragonite Ω ranges.
  • Formulas: G = rN, S = 1.80655 × Cl, % saturation, pH + pOH = 14, C₁V₁ = C₂V₂.
  • Your salinometer's calibration readings.

One sheet per team, both sides. Small, neat writing or a printed sheet fits a lot.

🦺 Practicing safely at home

  • Wear your goggles when you practice with the salinometer so they feel normal on tournament day.
  • Salt water stings eyes: rinse with plain water for a few minutes.
  • Don't drink your standards, and wash up after handling clay.
  • Wipe up spills; salty water on the floor is slippery.
