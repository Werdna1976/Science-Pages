The Dairy Lab

Food Science is about the chemistry of milk. At the tournament you'll make a dairy product (ice cream, curds, or butter) against the clock, hand in a notebook of experiments you ran at home, and take a written test. This page explains the science behind each one and has lab exhibits you can play with to figure out how to win.

How the event works

There are three scored parts. The notebook is the only part you can finish before you walk in the door, so it's the easiest place to lock in points.

1. Experiment notebook (before the tournament)

Run at least 3 experiments for each of the 3 lab tasks (9 total) and record them on the official Food Science Experiment Form. Change only one variable (one thing) per experiment. Only the first 3 per task are graded, 2 points each, so 18 points max. The notebook is impounded: you hand it in at check-in and get it back at the end, so you can't use it during the event.

2. Lab tasks (at the tournament)

You won't know which task you'll get until you're there. Regionals and invitationals usually run 1 task, states 1 or 2, nationals 2 or 3. So practice all three until they're automatic.

  • Ice cream: 0.1 point per millimeter of height of a scoop that stands on its own for 5 seconds. So a 60 mm scoop (about 2⅓ inches) = 6 points.
  • Curds and butter: compared to the supervisor's own batch. Match their mass and you get 10, the max. Half their mass gets 5.

3. Written test

Dairy science: milk chemistry, fats, sugars, proteins, vitamins and minerals, emulsions, pasteurization, probiotics, types of dairy products, lactose intolerance and allergies, labels, and health benefits. Score is your percent correct ÷ 2, so 100% = 50 points and 70% = 35 points. That's the biggest chunk by far, which is why there's a whole test topics section below.

Bring and wear

Category C goggles (the kind with covered air vents, stamped "Z87"), a lab coat or apron, skin covered neck to wrist to toes, long hair tied back. One 8.5×11" note sheet each (both sides), up to two calculators (no phones or watches). Your own pint bag, gallon bag, 500–600 mL lidded container, and a ~30 cm square of cheesecloth. Losing points for cleanup (up to 10%) or safety is the easiest way to throw away a medal. Full list in the checklist.

Task 1: Ice cream Freezing point depression

Goal: the tallest scoop that stands on its own for 5 seconds. Height wins, not taste.

The procedure

  1. Mix up to 125 mL milk, cream, or half and half with up to 15 mL sugar and up to 1.2 mL vanilla (optional).
  2. Half-fill the gallon bag with ice and add up to 90 mL salt.
  3. Seal the mix in the pint bag, put it inside the gallon bag, seal, and shake hard.
  4. After about 5–10 minutes, take out the ice cream.
  5. The scoop must stand for 5 seconds before it's measured. You're allowed to shape it by hand.

Kitchen measures: 125 mL ≈ ½ cup, 15 mL = 1 tablespoon, 1.2 mL ≈ ¼ teaspoon, 90 mL = 6 tablespoons. A pint bag is the small zip-top bag; a gallon bag is the big one.

The science

Pure ice sits at 0 °C. When salt dissolves in the thin layer of meltwater on the ice, the dissolved ions get in the way of water molecules locking back into crystals. Ice keeps melting but can't refreeze as easily, and melting absorbs heat, so the salty slush drops well below 0 °C, down to about −21 °C at the limit. That's freezing point depression, a colligative property: it depends on how many particles are dissolved, not what they are.

Salt makes ice melt. Melting needs energy, and the ice steals that energy as heat from whatever is touching it, including your bag of ice cream mix. It's like how sweat cools you off: as it evaporates, it pulls heat out of your skin. Salty ice gets much colder than plain ice, cold enough to freeze the mix fast.

Scientists write it as a formula:

ΔTf = i · Kf · m

  • ΔTf ("delta T f"): how many degrees lower the freezing point goes. Δ (delta) is the Greek letter scientists use for "change in."
  • i: how many pieces each unit breaks into when it dissolves. Salt (NaCl) splits into Na⁺ and Cl⁻, so i ≈ 2. Sugar doesn't split, so i = 1.
  • Kf: a number for water, 1.86 °C·kg/mol. It's always the same for water.
  • m: molality, how much stuff is dissolved: moles of salt per kilogram of water.

One mole of salt is 58.4 g (about 3 tablespoons). Dissolve it in 1 kg (1 liter) of water:
ΔTf = 2 × 1.86 × 1 = 3.7 °C, so the water now freezes at −3.7 °C instead of 0 °C.
The slush around the ice is much saltier than that, which is why salty ice can reach −10 to −20 °C.

The sugar in your mix does the same thing on a smaller scale, so sweeter mixes freeze at a lower temperature and come out softer.

What the exhibit is showing you

Height comes from volume and stiffness

Volume means how much space it takes up. Stiffness means how well it holds its shape. A scoop is basically a cone. For the same volume, a stiff mix can be shaped into a tall, skinny tower; a soft one slumps into a pancake. So you want all the mix you're allowed (volume) and a mix that is frozen enough to hold its shape (stiffness). Fat helps: fat droplets partly clump together during shaking and build a network that traps air and holds the shape.

Watch out for these traps

  • Not enough salt: the ice stays near 0 °C and the mix never freezes in time.
  • Heavy cream + lots of hard shaking: you start making butter. The texture turns grainy and greasy.
  • Too much sugar: lowers the mix's freezing point, so it stays soft longer.
  • Warm hands: hold the bag with a towel or gloves. Your hands are heat sources.
  • Salty leaks: wipe the pint bag before opening it so brine doesn't drip into the ice cream.

Notebook experiment ideas

  • Salt amount: 30 vs 60 vs 90 mL (measure brine temperature with a thermometer every minute).
  • Base: milk vs half and half vs cream (same salt, same time). Measure height.
  • Shake time: 5 vs 7.5 vs 10 minutes.
  • Salt type: table vs kosher vs rock salt at the same volume.
  • Sugar: 5 vs 10 vs 15 mL.
  • Shaping technique: free-poured vs packed into a cup and flipped vs hand-shaped cone.

Task 2: Curd mass Proteins & pH

Goal: the heaviest pile of curds from 120 mL of an unknown milk. You're scored against the supervisor's own result.

The procedure

  1. Weigh 120 mL of milk in a beaker. It should be near room temperature.
  2. Add drops of vinegar or lemon juice, stir for 30 seconds, and let it stand about 5 minutes.
  3. Pour through cheesecloth over a beaker and drain for 15 minutes.
  4. Weigh the curds.

The milk is an "unknown," meaning nobody tells you what kind it is. It could be skim, 2%, whole, or something else. Practice with all of them, and see how to tell milks apart.

What's pH? pH measures how acidic something is, on a scale from 0 to 14. Lower = more acidic (sour). 7 is neutral, like pure water. Each step is 10 times stronger, so pH 4 is ten times more acidic than pH 5.

Lemon juice ≈ 2 · vinegar ≈ 2.5 · orange juice ≈ 3.5 · milk ≈ 6.7 · water = 7 · baking soda ≈ 9

The science

About 80% of milk protein is casein. Casein clusters into tiny balls called micelles that carry a negative charge on their surface, so they repel each other and stay floating. Milk's normal pH is about 6.7.

Acid adds H⁺ ions that cancel those negative charges. At pH 4.6, the isoelectric point of casein, the micelles have no net charge, stop repelling, and clump into curds. Fat gets trapped in the clumps. What drains through is whey: water, lactose, minerals, and whey proteins, which don't curdle from acid alone at room temperature.

Go past pH 4.6 and the casein starts picking up a positive charge, so the micelles begin repelling again and the curd gets smaller and rubbery. More acid is not always better.

Milk is full of tiny protein balls. They all carry the same electric charge, so they push each other away, like two magnets with the same ends facing. That keeps them spread out and the milk smooth. Acid cancels the charge. Once they stop pushing apart, they stick together into lumps: curds. Add way too much acid and they get the opposite charge and start pushing apart again.

Strategy

Moves that add grams

  • Find your drop count for each milk type at home, and write it on your note sheet. That's the single most useful thing you can bring.
  • Add acid slowly while stirring gently, and stop once the liquid around the curds turns clear and yellow-green (that's whey).
  • Fold the cheesecloth into 2–4 layers so small curds don't escape.
  • Don't squeeze the curds. The water held inside them counts toward your mass.
  • Scrape the beaker and the cloth. Every curd left behind is lost points.

Why fattier milk gives heavier curds

Fat globules don't curdle themselves, but they get trapped inside the casein network as it forms, and so does water. Whole milk and half and half give heavier curds than skim even though skim has slightly more casein per cup. If the unknown milk gives a skimpy, rubbery curd, it's probably skim.

Real-world connection: paneer, ricotta, and queso blanco are all made this way, with acid plus heat. Most other cheeses use the enzyme rennet (chymosin) instead of acid.

Notebook experiment ideas

  • Drops of vinegar: 20 vs 60 vs 100 drops (same milk). Measure pH with strips if you can.
  • Vinegar vs lemon juice at the same number of drops.
  • Milk type: skim vs 2% vs whole.
  • Milk temperature: fridge-cold vs room temperature vs warm (about 40 °C).
  • Stirring: gentle vs vigorous.
  • Cheesecloth layers: 1 vs 2 vs 4.

Task 3: Butter from cream Emulsions

Goal: the most butter from 150 mL of heavy cream, shaken in your own 500–600 mL container. Scored against the supervisor's result.

The procedure

  1. Weigh 150 mL of heavy cream in your 500–600 mL container. The cream should be about 13 °C (55 °F).
  2. Cap it and shake until butter grains clearly separate from the liquid.
  3. Pour off the liquid (buttermilk) and weigh the butter.

The science

Cream is an oil-in-water emulsion: tiny fat globules floating in water, each wrapped in a thin skin called the milk fat globule membrane (made of phospholipids and proteins). The membrane keeps globules from merging.

Shaking whips in air. The fat globules line up around the bubbles (that's whipped cream). Keep shaking and the partly solid fat crystals poke holes in the membranes, liquid fat leaks out and glues globules together, the foam collapses, and the emulsion flips into a water-in-oil emulsion: butter, plus the leftover buttermilk. This flip is called phase inversion.

Picture cream as millions of tiny oil droplets, each wrapped in a thin skin, floating in water. Shaking tosses them around until their skins tear. The oily insides leak out and glue the droplets together into bigger and bigger clumps. Finally the clumps join into one big lump of fat with little drops of water trapped inside. That's butter. The water and everything else that didn't join the lump pours off as buttermilk.

Strategy

Listen to the jar

At first you'll hear sloshing. When the cream whips, the jar goes quiet because the foam doesn't move. Keep going. When you hear sloshing again, the foam has broken and butter is forming. Within a minute you should see yellow grains in thin, bluish-white buttermilk. That sound change is your best timer.

Why about 13 °C?

At 13 °C milk fat is a mix of solid crystals and liquid oil. The crystals poke through membranes (fast churning), and the liquid fat glues globules together. Too cold and everything is solid, so it takes forever. Too warm and the fat is so liquid that more of it stays in the buttermilk, and the butter is soft and greasy. Bring a thermometer and an insulated bag, and know what to do if your cream is too warm or cold.

Notebook experiment ideas

  • Cream temperature: 5 °C vs 13 °C vs 22 °C. Record time to butter and butter mass.
  • Container size or fill level: 150 mL in a 250 mL jar vs a 550 mL jar.
  • Shaking style: up-and-down vs side-to-side vs circular.
  • Cream brand or fat percent: whipping (30%) vs heavy (36%+).
  • Adding a clean marble (or not) to the jar as an agitator. Check whether your tournament allows it first.

Written test topics 50 points

The test is worth more than all the lab tasks combined. Here is the core of each topic, plus flashcards at the end.

Milk in one minute. Milk is mostly water (about 88%). Floating in it are four main things: fat (tiny droplets, which is why milk looks white and creamy), protein (mostly casein, the part that becomes curds and cheese), sugar (lactose), and minerals like calcium. Almost every dairy product is made by separating, concentrating, or changing one of those parts. Cream is milk with extra fat. Butter is almost all fat. Cheese is mostly protein and fat with the water squeezed out. Yogurt is milk where bacteria turned some lactose into acid.

Milk chemistry

Proteins

Milk is about 3.3% protein: casein (about 80%) and whey proteins (about 20%, mostly β-lactoglobulin and α-lactalbumin). Casein forms micelles held together partly by calcium phosphate and curdles with acid (pH 4.6) or the enzyme rennet/chymosin, which snips κ-casein (κ is the Greek letter "kappa"), the "hairy" outer layer that keeps micelles apart. Whey proteins stay dissolved in acid but denature (unfold) with heat, which is why heated milk forms a skin.

Test for protein: biuret reagent turns violet.

Carbohydrates

The sugar in milk is lactose, a disaccharide of glucose + galactose joined by a β(1→4) bond (β is the Greek letter "beta"; the numbers say which atoms of each sugar link up). Sucrose (table sugar) is glucose + fructose. Starches are long chains of glucose, which is how thickeners in some ice creams work.

Test for reducing sugars (lactose, glucose): Benedict's solution turns green → orange → brick red when heated. Sucrose is not a reducing sugar. Test for starch: iodine turns blue-black.

Fats (lipids)

Milk fat is mostly triglycerides: a glycerol backbone + 3 fatty acids, joined by esterification (each bond releases a water molecule; the reverse, hydrolysis, adds water back). Saturated fatty acids have no C=C double bonds, pack tightly, and are solid at room temperature. Unsaturated ones have C=C bonds that kink the chain, so they're liquid. Butterfat is about 2/3 saturated. Essential fatty acids (linoleic, omega-6; α-linolenic, omega-3) must come from food.

Phospholipids (like lecithin) have a water-loving head and fat-loving tails, which makes them great emulsifiers.

Emulsions

An emulsion is tiny droplets of one liquid spread through another they don't mix with. Milk and cream are oil-in-water. Butter is water-in-oil (the inverse). Emulsifiers sit at the boundary and keep droplets from merging. Homogenization forces milk through tiny holes at high pressure to shrink fat globules so cream doesn't rise to the top. Ice cream is an emulsion and a foam (air) and a suspension of ice crystals.

Shake oil and vinegar salad dressing: it mixes for a moment, then separates. That's an emulsion that falls apart. Add a little mustard or egg yolk (emulsifiers) and it stays mixed. Milk stays mixed because the proteins and phospholipids around each fat droplet act like that mustard.

Lactose intolerance vs milk allergy

Lactose intolerance is digestive: the body makes too little of the enzyme lactase, so lactose reaches the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it into gas. Uncomfortable, not dangerous. Lactose-free milk has lactase added, which splits lactose into glucose + galactose (it tastes sweeter). Yogurt and aged cheese have less lactose.

Milk allergy is the immune system reacting to milk proteins (casein or whey). It can be serious. Milk is one of the 9 major U.S. food allergens that must be declared on labels.

Labels

Nutrition Facts must list calories, fats, sugars (including added sugars), protein, calcium, vitamin D, potassium, and iron. Milk names: whole (~3.25% fat), reduced-fat (2%), low-fat (1%), fat-free/skim (under 0.5 g fat per serving).

Effects of cooking

  • Skin on hot milk: whey proteins denature and casein dries at the surface.
  • Browning: the Maillard reaction between lactose and proteins (dulce de leche, caramel flavors).
  • Curdling in sauces: acid + heat (tomato soup with milk) pushes casein toward pH 4.6.
  • Scorching: proteins and lactose stick and burn on the pan bottom.

Preservation

  • Refrigeration slows bacteria.
  • Pasteurization kills pathogens.
  • Fermentation (acid) keeps yogurt and cheese safe.
  • Drying (powdered milk) removes the water bacteria need.
  • Salt in butter and cheese draws water away from microbes.

The experiment notebook 18 points

These are the only points you can lock in before the tournament, and doing the experiments is also the best practice for the lab tasks.

What a full-credit experiment has

  • A question and a hypothesis ("If I use more salt, the ice cream will be taller, because…").
  • One independent variable (what you change), the dependent variable (what you measure), and the controlled variables (everything you keep the same).
  • Exact amounts, temperatures, and times. Someone else should be able to repeat it.
  • A data table with units, ideally with more than one trial.
  • A conclusion that says whether the hypothesis was right and explains why using the science.

Use the official Experiment Form. Only the first 3 per task are graded, so put your best ones first.

Say you want to know if more salt makes taller ice cream. Salt is the independent variable (the one thing you change). Height is the dependent variable (what you measure, because it "depends" on the salt). Milk, sugar, ice, and shake time are controlled variables: you keep them the same so salt is the only possible reason the height changed.

Game plan

The practical stuff: how to practice, how to split up the work with your partner, and what to put on your note sheet.

🗓 A practice schedule

8+ weeks out: Read this page. Do each task once in the kitchen just to see how it goes. Gather gear from the checklist.
6–8 weeks out: Run your 9 notebook experiments (3 per task) and fill in the Experiment Forms. Do about 2 per week.
4–6 weeks out: Start test study: one topic section per week plus flashcards. Take a practice test from Scio.ly.
2–4 weeks out: Time yourselves doing each task start to finish, including cleanup. Make your note sheet.
Last week: One timed run of each task. Pack your kit. Review flashcards.

👯 Splitting 50 minutes between two partners

You can work on the lab and the test at the same time. A plan many teams use:

  • Partner A starts the lab task right away (it has waiting time built in: shaking, standing, draining).
  • Partner B starts the test, doing the questions they're surest of first.
  • During waiting times (curds standing 5 min and draining 15 min), A joins the test.
  • Save the last 5 minutes for cleanup and checking that every test answer is filled in. A blank answer is always wrong; a guess might be right.

Decide who does what before the tournament, and practice it that way.

📄 What to put on your note sheet

  • Your drop counts for each milk type (from your curd experiments).
  • Your best ice cream recipe: base, sugar, salt amount, shake time.
  • The butter stages and the "jar goes quiet, then sloshes" tip.
  • Formulas: ΔTf = i·Kf·m and °F = °C × 9/5 + 32.
  • Tables you'll forget: pasteurization times, vitamins and deficiencies, bacteria in each cultured product, pH of common foods.
  • Structures: lactose = glucose + galactose; a triglyceride drawing.

One 8.5×11" sheet per person, both sides. Two partners = four sides, so split the topics instead of copying the same notes twice.

🕵️ Telling unknown milks apart

  • Color: skim looks slightly bluish and see-through at the edges; whole and half & half look creamier and more yellow-white.
  • Swirl test: swirl it in a clear cup. Fattier milk leaves a thicker white coating on the sides.
  • Curds: skim gives small, rubbery curds; whole milk gives bigger, softer ones.
  • Powdered milk (mixed with water) may have tiny undissolved bits and tastes "cooked." The supervisor might use it.

Never taste anything at the tournament unless the supervisor says it's allowed.

🦺 Practicing safely at home

  • Wear your goggles when practicing, so they feel normal on tournament day.
  • Vinegar or lemon juice in your eyes stings: rinse with water for several minutes.
  • Salty ice gets cold enough to hurt skin. Use a towel or gloves.
  • Keep cream and milk in the fridge until you use them, and don't eat things that sat out for hours.
  • Ask an adult before using the stove for any heating experiments.
